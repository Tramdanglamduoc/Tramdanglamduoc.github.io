I've always been fascinated by systems: how simple parts, connected the right way, give rise to behavior that looks like intelligence. I studied Computer Systems to understand how machines are built from the ground up, from hardware, software, and networks to the logic that holds them together. Along the way, I realized that the most intricate system I could ever hope to study isn't a machine at all. It's the human body.

Biomedicine sits where computation, physics, biology, and clinical practice meet. There, a well-designed model can change how a surgeon feels tissue through a robotic arm, or help a researcher find the one paper that matters among thousands. Biomedical engineering is where all of these come together into something you can build, and it is the field I'm now moving into.

Today, I work at the intersection of artificial intelligence, medicine, and open knowledge. I build simulation tools, data pipelines, and retrieval systems that help clinicians and researchers see further. Step by step, I'm moving from writing software about the body to engineering systems that work directly with it: sensors, devices, and living tissue. The system I keep coming back to is the human brain: how it senses, signals, and heals, and how engineering can step in when something goes wrong.

I believe good tools shouldn't belong only to well-funded labs. I hope to carry that belief through a master's and PhD in biomedical engineering and, one day, into a company that turns research into care people can actually reach. For now, my main work is learning to build intelligent systems that serve the most complex system of all: us.
